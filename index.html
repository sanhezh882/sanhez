How work moves through the studio.

Five stages, with a decision and an artefact at the end of each. No stage begins before the one beneath it is signed off.

Stage one

Definition

Reading, interviewing and mapping the work as it is actually done — including the exceptions nobody documents. Scope, risks and trade-offs land in one document you can argue with.

Leaves you with A written scope, a risk list, and an honest estimate.

Stage two

Architecture

The shape of the system before the first line of production code: boundaries, data ownership, failure modes, and what we deliberately choose not to build.

Leaves you with A diagram, a set of decisions, and their reasons.

Stage three

Build

Two-week increments, each ending in something you can open and use. Review and re-planning happen inside the increment, not after it closes.

Leaves you with A working build every fourteen days, and a changelog you can read.

Stage four

Hardening

Load, security, accessibility and recovery tested against the conditions of real use. Then a handover, so your team can run what we built.

Leaves you with Test results, runbooks, and a handover that was rehearsed.

Stage five

Operation

Monitoring, maintenance and improvement under an agreed response standard, for as long as the system is needed — and until someone else can take it on.

Leaves you with A system that is watched, maintained, and documented.

Engineering standards

The rules we do not trade away.

Every project is different. These do not change with the project, the deadline, or the size of the budget.

Readable before clever

A stranger should be able to follow a payment path in twenty minutes. If they cannot, the work is not finished — however impressive the code looks.

Tested where failure is expensive

Coverage follows consequence, not a percentage target. The checkout path and the audit log get attention because of what breaks if they fail.

Observable from the first release

Logs, metrics and traces ship with the feature. A system that cannot tell you why it failed is not yet in production, whatever the deployment says.

Secure at the boundary

Least privilege by default, input validated where it enters, dependencies kept current, secrets kept out of the repository.

If we cannot explain a decision to the person who will maintain it, we have not finished deciding.

Three ways to work together.

The studio

A small studio, deliberately. Exact work, repeated until it is ordinary.

Wuan North Gate Restaurant is a software development and services company. We design and build custom systems, engineer the platforms they run on, and stay after launch to operate them.

Teams are kept small and senior. The people who scope the work are the people who write the code — and usually the people who answer when something goes wrong at eleven at night.

The name was earned in another trade, and we have kept it. What carried across is the habit: measured ingredients, no shortcuts, and a kitchen you would be willing to show a customer.

We take on fewer projects than we are asked to. That is on purpose. It is what keeps the standard where it is.

What we don’t do

  • Staff a project we cannot supervise.
  • Promise a date before we have read the work.
  • Hand over a system without the notes needed to run it.
  • Call something finished because the invoice has been sent.
